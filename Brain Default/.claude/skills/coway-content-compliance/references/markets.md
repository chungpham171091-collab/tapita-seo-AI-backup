## Germany

**Framework:** Heilmittelwerbegesetz (HWG) §11, Gesetz gegen den unlauteren Wettbewerb (UWG).
**Risk level: HIGH.** Not primarily because of regulators - because competitors and Abmahnvereine send cease-and-desist letters as routine business practice, and they are cheap to send and expensive to receive.

The decisive point about HWG: it reaches claims that imply the **detection, removal or relief of disease even for products that are not medical devices**. A German reader should not be able to finish a sentence of Coway copy with "...so I won't get ill."

Not permitted:
- Any claim implying detection, removal, alleviation or prevention of disease
- Fear-inducing depiction of illness - explicitly prohibited under HWG §11
- Virus or bacteria removal percentages without independent laboratory substantiation
- Imagery or wording suggesting a medical or clinical context

Permitted:
- General air quality improvement claims backed by certified test results
- Certified performance data (CADR, TÜV, VDE, AHAM) with source
- Dust, pollen and odour reduction, framed as air treatment

Extra caution: even a properly substantiated virus reduction figure can read as disease prevention in context. Coway's own terminology rule already resolves this - **state the particle size, never name the pathogen.** 0.01 micron is smaller than a virus or a bacterium, so the specification implies the capability without making the claim. In Germany that implication is the only safe form; naming the pathogen is an invitation to an Abmahnung even with a lab report in hand.

Language notes: formal address (Sie). German readers respond well to specification-led, measured copy, which happens to align with what the law allows.

## United Kingdom

**Framework:** CAP Code rule 12.1 (medicines, medical devices, health-related products), Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 (DMCCA).
**Risk level: HIGH.** The ASA has real enforcement precedent against air purifier advertising - upheld rulings and ad bans, published publicly under the brand's name. Reputational cost lands before any fine does.

Not permitted:
- Medical claims - "prevents infection", "treats symptoms", "relieves asthma" - without MHRA registration of the product as a medical device
- Virus or bacteria kill-rate percentages without documentary evidence held on file
- Claims linking the product to relief of a specific named condition
- Implied health outcomes dressed as lifestyle benefit ("wake up clearer", "breathe easier") - the ASA reads implication, not just literal wording

Permitted:
- "Cleaner air", "helps remove dust and pollen", non-medical framing with evidence on file
- Certified performance data with source
- Hay fever season as a *seasonal context* - "during pollen season" is a time of year; "for hay fever sufferers" is a condition claim

The DMCCA raises the stakes on misleading practices generally, including unclear pricing and unsubstantiated environmental or performance claims. Evidence must exist before publication, not be assembled after a complaint.

## France

**Framework:** Code de la consommation L.121-1 (pratiques commerciales trompeuses), ARPP health claims guidance.
**Risk level: HIGH.** Misleading commercial practice is a criminal offence - up to two years' imprisonment and €300,000 for individuals, with penalties scalable to a percentage of turnover. DGCCRF can impose administrative fines up to €75,000 on companies.

Not permitted:
- Positioning the product as medical - implying prevention, treatment or cure of human illness
- Citing pollution statistics without a verifiable source, particularly where the effect is to create fear
- Unverified efficacy figures of any kind

Permitted:
- Citing official sources - WHO/OMS, ANSES, ATMO regional air quality data - **but not tying them to product efficacy.** The statistic establishes context; it must not be arranged to suggest the product answers it.

The French trap is structural rather than lexical: a legally clean statistic followed immediately by a legally clean product claim can together constitute a misleading practice. Separate them. Do not let a paragraph run "ANSES reports X → Coway does Y".

Language notes: vouvoiement. French editorial tolerates less superlative than English; "purificateur d'air" copy should stay descriptive. The category noun "purificateur d'air" is correct and stays. Avoid *assainir* and the verb *purifier* only when they are attached to a health outcome or a person ("purifier l'air pour protéger votre famille"); "purifier l'air de la pièce" describes the air and is fine.

## Netherlands

**Framework:** Wet oneerlijke handelspraktijken (unfair commercial practices), enforced by ACM; Reclame Code Commissie (RCC) for advertising complaints.
**Risk level: MEDIUM.** ACM can issue corrective orders and fines; RCC can order an ad withdrawn and publishes its decisions.

Not permitted:
- Fear-based pressure language
- Disease prevention or relief claims
- Unverified efficacy figures

Permitted:
- General indoor air quality claims
- Verifiable specifications - CADR, coverage in m², noise in dB, filter class

Dutch consumers and the RCC are particularly unforgiving of pressure and exaggeration; plain, checkable copy performs better commercially as well as legally.

Language notes: informal "je" is normal in consumer copy, but keep the register sober. Dutch readers read superlatives as sales talk.

## Cross-market summary

| Rule | UK | DE | NL | FR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Named illness linked to product | No | No | No | No |
| Implied disease prevention or relief | No | No (broadest reading) | No | No |
| Unsourced efficacy % | No | No | No | No |
| Named virus/bacteria claim, even with a lab report | No (Coway rule) | No (Coway rule) | No (Coway rule) | No (Coway rule) |
| Fear framing | No | Explicitly banned (HWG §11) | No | No, esp. via statistics |
| Third-party statistics | Sourced | Sourced, no disease context | Sourced | Sourced, never bridged to product |
| Dust / pollen / odour reduction | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Certified data (CADR, TÜV) | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Practical risk | High - ASA precedent | High - Abmahnung | Medium - ACM/RCC | High - criminal |

The market sections describe what the law allows. Coway's internal rule is stricter and takes precedence: never name a pathogen, in any market, even with a lab report. State the particle size instead.

## Localisation notes beyond claims

Formality, casing, typography, numbers and dates for every locale are in `locale-packs.md`.

Never ship a machine-translated UK article into these markets without re-running the claim audit. Claim decisions do not survive translation - a sentence that passes CAP can breach HWG in identical wording, and a claim that was correctly qualified in English frequently loses its qualifier in the target language.
